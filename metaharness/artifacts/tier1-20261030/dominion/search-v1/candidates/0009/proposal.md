Hypothesis: complete-policy comparison can avoid two observed acquisition failures: speculative individual purchases under a mismatched continuation, and a fixed money plan that thins faster than it rebuilds. Candidate 0003's losing trace_seed1101.jsonl buys Village instead of Gold on turn 6 (line 18: projected margins +0.600 versus -0.667), and Market instead of Province on turn 8 (line 27: +1.413 versus -0.396). These are small simulated advantages, not evidence that the actual deck supports the detour. Candidate 0005 trace_seed1200.jsonl removes six Coppers by turn 7 and the last on turn 8; it makes no purchase on turns 6, 7, or 8 and does not buy Gold until turn 10. It finishes -7.

This candidate starts from 0005's declarative-policy idea and legal local execution, but replaces its fixed target list with a portfolio of complete acquisition policies. At each purchase, sampled public-information worlds compare buying AND continuing with each policy. A policy selects both the immediate purchase and the subsequent coherent build, instead of evaluating an isolated purchase under a single shared future. Policies differ structurally: single-terminal money, thin money, cantrip economy, and draw/action/payload engines. Every simulated policy optimizes final VP margin against the disclosed opponent. Sampling never reads a hidden order or reset seed.

Astra may propose one additional declarative target policy. Its output is bounded and evaluated alongside local policies, rather than directly overriding the executor. The request is asynchronous and never blocks game progress. Garbage, failure, or late advice leaves the local portfolio available. Local code owns prompt IDs, complete multi-card selections, and actual card effects. A minimum projected improvement is required to switch away from the current policy, reducing shuffle-noise churn.

Confirmation: complete games without errors/illegal actions, higher actual terminal mean margin than 0005, and trace evidence that policy comparison selects coherent economies with fewer unproductive build turns. Compare forecast margins with actual results; strong forecasts followed by poor games would refute the simulator or continuation model. A gain confined to one kingdom would support specialization only, not a general improvement. This is a structural policy-search experiment, not an assertion that its finite Monte Carlo estimates are exact.

Implementation details: the independent `Player`/`World` forward model and local card-choice routines are adapted from 0003, while 0005 supplies the declarative acquisition-policy design. This is not 0005's exact implementation with a new threshold: its planner/executor has been replaced by policy-level search. The portfolio includes unthinned one-terminal money, thin money, limited cantrip money, action/draw/payload engines with different build commitments, and a Chapel/Laboratory target-chain policy where available. Real purchases, simulated purchases, and constrained gains share `buy_values`; tie-breaking is identical for real and simulated purchases. The selected policy also supplies thinning and terminal-retention preferences.

At a purchase where policies disagree, all receive 32 common-random-number samples, then the best three plus incumbent receive up to 64 and 96 additional samples. Only surviving finalists may win. Switching requires a projected margin improvement greater than 0.75; this is a stability heuristic, not a statistical significance claim. Search caps each purchase at 14 seconds, scales down with remaining episode time, and disables below a 35-second reserve. If policies all recommend the same purchase, it executes without sampling. Forward games stop at a genuine pile ending or a 100-round computation cap. A cap score is an approximation, not a certified final result. Unknown supply cards disable forward search. Current-turn purchases after an ending condition is already reached prioritize additional exact VP, including Gardens boundaries.

Opponent hidden zones are sampled from public ownership and zone counts, without conditioning on the entire public discard history. Own unknown deck contents are ownership minus visible hand/discard/play. No actual hidden order, reset seed, game implementation, or external data is accessed. Finite sampling, approximate opponent beliefs, greedy nested choices, and a fixed continuation within each hypothetical game remain limitations. Policies with effectively identical behavior can occupy multiple search slots; a future iteration could deduplicate them more aggressively.

Validation: final preflight PASS (32 actions, +6 stand-in VP margin). Its short budget skips the model path. Seven direct regression tests pass, including all 1,822 archived pending prompts under money and engine plans, shared real/simulated acquisition selection, draw/cleanup conservation, Gardens scoring, hand-only Moat protection, and garbage/timeout/runtime/pending Astra responses. Model output only adds a bounded candidate; it never directly replaces the incumbent or supplies action IDs.

Exactly two permitted development evaluations were run. The first completed at +11/+9 in about 12 seconds per game, with zero errors or illegal actions. Reading its traces exposed a selection bug: a policy eliminated at 32 samples could re-enter the final choice against finalists with 192 samples. The final version restricts selection to survivors and aligns acquisition tie-breaking. The second development run (`dev/20261030-010816`) completed at +10/+9, mean +9.5, in 13.9/14.8 seconds, with zero errors, illegal actions, or truncations. Each requested one Astra call; neither answered before completion, and cancellation was clean. Therefore these results measure the local portfolio, not the value of model-proposed policies. Both dev episodes are on the same development kingdom, so they do not validate Chapel/Witch strategy quality. No full training evaluation or held-out result was accessed.

`forecast_audit.json` contains synthetic continuation forecasts from three frozen archived public observations, not additional actual-game evaluations. It was generated before the final optional Lab-chain addition. It suggests the portfolio can distinguish money-favorable and curse-engine-favorable situations, but does not validate forecast calibration. The two dev runs support reliable execution; the main hypothesis remains for the evaluator to test across training kingdoms.
