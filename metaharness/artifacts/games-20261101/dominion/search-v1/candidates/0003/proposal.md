Hypothesis: a public-belief rollout controller can turn the available wall time into completed Dominion games and improve acquisition decisions against the disclosed opponents. The baseline's per-prompt inference spends most of the episode budget resolving low-branching choices. Merely shortening prompts cannot address the fixed model latency; purchases should instead be evaluated through cheap sampled continuations, with Astra advising on strategic continuation policies at no more than two checkpoints.

Evidence: candidate 0000's eval.json records all six episodes ending at the 1,800-second deadline, zero completed wins, and only 22.33 steps on average. In trace_seed1301.jsonl, the model returned the legal JSON form `{"decision_id":10,"option_ids":["3","0","1","2"]}`, which the generic parser reduced to invalid `choose`; the next call took 242.291 seconds to produce `choose 10 3`. The episode finally stopped at decision 30 on turn 13 with -14 VP, six Provinces still available, and an unfinished thinning/engine deck. The archived final state explicitly has Sentry and Witch available; getting to the next ordinary action should not require another slow model round trip.

This is a fresh implementation, not a revision of the leader's one-action-per-call recipe. It has a forward model based solely on published rules, public ownership, observed zones, supply text/costs, and the disclosed opponent policies. At each buy prompt it samples unknown orders and opponent zones, evaluates legal acquisitions through complete game continuations, and successively narrows candidates by mean terminal VP margin. It does not access the simulator, seed, hidden hand, RNG, or any archive at runtime. Astra gets the current observation plus 96-world forecasts for the available continuation archetypes at opening and, if the first request has completed, a midgame checkpoint. It selects a bounded continuation archetype and scoring/thinning policy. Until advice arrives, the best forecast supplies the default. A payload floor prevents the continuation policy from endlessly buying draw cards in a deck that cannot produce $8 even when fully drawn. Code executes action sequencing, trashing, discards, reactions, gains, legality checks and end-to-end progress. Structured advice is never executable code and malformed advice leaves a functional default policy.

The key experimental distinction is search over future public-belief games rather than delegating each choice or simply caching a purchase list. Searches use independent samples, share sampled initial worlds across alternatives, and are limited to nine seconds per purchase. Model requests use 180-second inference timeouts, but admission/queueing can exceed that limit. A background request receives at most a 60-second local opening wait; thereafter advice is polled only between purchases and never mutates a running search. Pending calls are cancelled by the evaluator at game end, as the contract permits. Completed-call failures are caught and retain the local policy. Unknown supply cards disable the forward model. Every posted selection is assembled from the current prompt's occurrence IDs and bounds.

Confirmation: completed games with no illegal actions/errors; substantially fewer model calls than the baseline; positive mean final margin; rollout-selected purchases whose eventual decks support the intended policy. Refutation: completed but losing games despite sufficient time; systematic simulator bias (especially shuffle timing, reactions, or terminal collisions); excessive variance in root purchase rankings; search choosing unsupported or incoherent acquisitions. Search values and sample counts are traced for diagnosis. The forward model is an approximation of unknown card locations and continuation behavior, not an assertion of hidden state.

Development evidence: the first permitted dev traces show opening advice becoming usable at episode clocks 577.21 and 653.90 seconds, even though recorded model inference latencies were only 10.50 and 12.93 seconds. Both selected Gardens despite the implemented rush having roughly -13 to -15 forecast margins. This justified the bounded local wait and showing policy forecasts to Astra. The first run still used synchronous advice; the second and final permitted dev run tests the revised asynchronous controller. No training or held-out evaluation was run.

Validation so far: preflight passed after the payload-floor change (64 steps, game_done, no error); all 140 archived prompt selections met bounds and occurrence-ID validity. Local tests cover garbage advice, model exceptions, a pending future, bounded parameters, shuffle timing, Gardens arithmetic, Moat hand-only protection, and thinning safeguards. Ownership/zone conservation and nonnegative piles held for 10,710 sampled forward-model turns. These are correctness checks, not evidence that sampled continuations predict real game outcomes. Final dev results will be recorded below.

Final permitted dev-eval result (seeds 3100 and 3101): both episodes completed in 92.52 s and 91.03 s, with no errors, no illegal actions, no failed System-2 calls, and one advice call each. Margins were +9 and -2 (mean +3.5); the first game won by empty Province, the second lost by empty Province. The first run used the older synchronous opening advice and stopped at 14–17 steps while advice was queued. The second run used the asynchronous controller and completed 56 and 44 steps. It selected money-policy forecasts at the opening because the measured Gardens forecast was -13.27 and -15.19, then acquired Provinces and ended normally. Additional local resampling of a losing episode's observed decision found a clear rollout ranking (Market -1.446 ± 0.106, Silver -2.674 ± 0.106, pass -4.417 ± 0.111, 4,096 worlds each), so the remaining loss is strategic rather than apparent root-ranking noise. The code now gives every candidate a common initial sample pool before refinement.
